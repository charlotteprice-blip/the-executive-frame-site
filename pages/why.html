Three sciences sit at the centre of the architecture. A wider body of clinical and applied work shapes how they translate into practice — integrated where each tradition meaningfully strengthens the system, not stacked as a literature review.

Cognitive & behavioural traditions CBT · DBT · ACT +

Three of the most replicated frameworks in clinical psychology. Each contributes a different lever — restructuring distorted thinking, holding contradiction without collapsing, and moving toward what matters even in difficult internal weather.

Self-compassion research Neff · Gilbert +

The work showing that warmth toward oneself is not a softening of standards but a precondition for sustainable performance. Compassion as a regulatory tool, not a sentiment.

Cognitive architecture Executive function research +

Decades of research into the systems that organise thinking — what they cost, what supports them, and how they degrade under sustained load. The structural reality behind clarity.

Neuroscience & positive psychology Foundations · long-arc view +

Contemporary neuroscience grounds the architecture in how the brain processes emotion, attention, motivation, and load. Positive psychology contributes the long-arc view: strengths, flourishing, and the conditions under which capacity expands rather than depletes.

The result is a framework that does not borrow from these traditions selectively. It integrates what works — where it works — into one operating system.

Centre · regulatory base
Physiological sigh T

Cyclic sighing produces the greatest reduction in physiological arousal among tested breathwork interventions. Two short inhales through the nose followed by a long, slow exhale.

Balban et al. (2023); Huberman Lab, Stanford School of Medicine

Polyvagal theory T

The ventral vagal complex mediates safety signalling. Extended exhalation engages vagal braking — a primary mechanism of regulation.

Porges (2011). The Polyvagal Theory.

TIPP / dive reflex T

Temperature change and paired muscle relaxation as distress tolerance skills. The mammalian dive reflex offers a fast physiological reset.

Linehan (1993). DBT Skills Training Manual.

Interoception T

Bodily signal awareness is the substrate of emotional regulation and emotional granularity — the precision with which feelings are named.

Barrett (2017); Craig (2009)

Neurosequential model T

Regulate before Relate before Reason. The biological order is non-negotiable in trauma and developmental contexts.

Perry (2006); Perry & Szalavitz (2017)

Panoramic vision T

Wide visual field activates parasympathetic tone via autonomic pathways linked to visual processing — a fast shift out of sympathetic dominance.

Huberman Lab; applied neurobiology

Clarify · cognitive substrate
Cognitive restructuring T

Identifying and revising distorted automatic thoughts. The foundational mechanism of cognitive therapy — separating fact from interpretation.

Beck (1976, 1995). Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders.

Working memory & executive function T

Working memory, inhibition, and attention as the cognitive systems that organise thought into action. Pressure narrows what these systems can hold.

Barkley (2012). Executive Functions.

Cognitive load theory T

Working memory has hard limits. Sequencing, chunking, and external scaffolding reduce load and restore accuracy under demand.

Sweller (1988, 2011). Cognitive Load Theory.

Fact vs story T

Separating sensory data from narrative interpretation. A core skill of cognitive therapy and the architecture of clear thinking under load.

Beck (1976); applied across CBT and ACT.

Attention reorientation T

Voluntary redirection of attention as a regulatory and cognitive skill. Trained, not assumed — and depleted by sustained demand.

Posner & Petersen (1990); attentional control research.

Choose · agency substrate
Self-Determination Theory T

Autonomy, competence, and relatedness as the three basic psychological needs. The most-replicated motivational framework in psychology.

Deci & Ryan (2000). Intrinsic and extrinsic motivations.

Values-based action (ACT) T

Moving toward what matters in the presence of difficult internal experience. Values clarification as a behavioural anchor.

Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson (1999, 2011). Acceptance and Commitment Therapy.

Behavioural activation T

Action precedes feeling. Graded, deliberate engagement rebuilds capacity that withdrawal degrades.

Lewinsohn (1974); Martell, Dimidjian & Herman-Dunn (2010).

Microsteps & commitment planning T

Small, specific, value-aligned actions repeated across time. Behaviour change tends to compound through structure, not willpower.

Self-determination and behavioural-activation literatures.

Narrative therapy T

Identity and story as the architecture of sustained change. Values-aligned action across time becomes the architecture of a coherent self.

White & Epston (1990). Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends.
