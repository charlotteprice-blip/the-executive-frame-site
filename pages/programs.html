01 — The Premise

Most breakdowns in capable lives are not effort problems.
They are sequence problems.

Capable people do not fail under sustained load because they lack effort, intelligence, or resolve. They fail because the system narrows.

Modern life imposes four loads simultaneously: cognitive load (the thinking work, the decisions, the planning), emotional load (the work of holding others, the work of holding oneself), structural load (deadlines, demands, pressure), and identity load — the gap between the person you intended to be and the person the conditions are producing.

Under sustained four-load conditions, the nervous system enters a predictable cascade. The work is not to override this. It is to know it, name it, and have a sequence ready when it arrives.

02 — The Architecture

Centre. Clarify. Choose.

Three stages. Run in order. The order is the intervention.

Stage 01 · Centre

Stabilise the system enough to think.

Regulation before cognition. Not calm — regulated enough to access the prefrontal cortex. The somatic work that brings the body back into a state where thinking is available again.

Without it, every move that follows is compromised.
Stage 02 · Clarify

Restore cognitive accuracy.

Once the system stabilises, thinking becomes available — but it is biased toward speed and certainty. Not more thinking. More accurate thinking. Fact, story, triage. The discipline that protects sound decisions.

Fact, story, triage. The discipline of accuracy.
Stage 03 · Choose

Act with intention.

From regulation and clarity, action becomes available — and deliberate. One step. Aligned. Owned. Not perfect, not exhaustive, but consistent with the person you intend to be under pressure.

The loop closes on action. Then it begins again.

06 — The mechanisms

The mechanisms are forty years old.

No new science. Disciplined adaptation of established clinical and cognitive research to the specific conditions of capable lives under load.

Balban et al. (2023) · Cell Reports Medicine +

The Physiological Sigh (cyclic sighing) demonstrated as the most effective brief breathing intervention for autonomic arousal reduction. Used in Module 2.

Beck (1976), Burns (1980) · Cognitive Distortions +

The five cognitive distortions most often present in legal cognition under load — catastrophising, mind-reading, all-or-nothing, personalisation, should-statements. Used in Module 3.

Jacobson (1996), Dimidjian et al. (2006) · Behavioural Activation +

One of the most evidence-based behavioural interventions in clinical psychology. Action precedes motivation. Used in Module 4.

Arnsten (2009) · Nature Reviews Neuroscience +

Stress-pathway impairment of prefrontal cortex function. Working memory narrows from 7±2 to 2–3 items under sustained threat. Used throughout the programme.

Baddeley & Hitch (1974), Baddeley (2003) · Working Memory +

The working memory model that grounds the Now / Do / Later triage in Module 3 — capacity is finite, and triage is a competence, not a personality trait.

Mehrabian (1971) · Communication channels +

The widely misunderstood but core finding on multi-channel communication. In written legal practice, tone is the entire channel. Used in Module 4.

Leroy (2009) · Attention Residue +

The cognitive cost of unfinished tasks on subsequent capacity. Grounds the file-finish discipline and the closing-out practice. Used in Modules 3 and 5.

LexisNexis NZ Wellbeing Report (2023) · Sector data +

72% of NZ lawyers report high or very high stress. 45% identify sustained pressure as the primary driver of professional quality lapses. The conditions that produced the programme.
